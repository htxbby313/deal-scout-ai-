Status: ownership recorded; commercial customer terms remain a draft and require legal review.

The proprietary notice in `LICENSE` identifies the owner without inventing jurisdiction or final customer-contract terms.

## Intended model

Deal Scout AI should remain proprietary software and be offered under a commercial subscription license. Customers receive a limited, non-exclusive, non-transferable right to use the hosted product during a paid subscription. Source code, research connectors, scoring logic, branding, and derivative-product rights remain with the copyright owner.

## Recommended license tiers

| Tier | Intended user | Suggested boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Owner | Individual investor | One named user and one workspace |
| Team | Acquisition company | Multiple named users in one company workspace |
| Enterprise | Larger operator | Negotiated users, data retention, support, and integrations |

## Terms the final agreement must cover

- legal copyright owner and licensor name;
- customer and authorized-user definitions;
- subscription term, fees, renewal, suspension, and termination;
- permitted use and restrictions on resale, sublicensing, scraping the service, reverse engineering, and competitive model extraction;
- ownership of customer-entered data versus Deal Scout software and derived system metadata;
- third-party listing, map, government-data, and photo rights and attribution;
- no guarantee that listing, title, zoning, flood, utility, contact, comparable-sale, or map data is complete or current;
- customer duty to verify acquisition decisions and comply with real-estate, privacy, telemarketing, and outreach laws;
- confidentiality, security, warranties, liability limits, indemnity, governing law, and dispute terms;
- export, assignment, and survival clauses.

Confirm whether Mississippi should also govern the customer subscription agreement. The company's Mississippi formation jurisdiction is recorded above, but formation and contract governing law are separate legal facts.

## Source notes

The U.S. Copyright Office treats computer programs as copyrightable works and publishes separate registration guidance for computer programs. Copyright ownership and any work-made-for-hire or assignment history should be confirmed before the licensor is named. A qualified attorney should review the final commercial agreement.
